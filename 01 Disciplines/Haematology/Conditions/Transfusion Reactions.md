# Presentation Questions
- What's being transfused, when did it start and how much has gone in?
- Has the transfusion been stopped?
- Observations compared with their pre-transfusion baseline, including temperature?
- Symptoms?
	- Rash or itch
	- Dyspnoea, wheeze or stridor
	- Rigors, or chest, back or flank pain
	- Dark urine
- Reason for the transfusion? (Are they still bleeding and likely to need more blood?)
- Heart failure, renal impairment or positive fluid balance? ⇒ risk of TACO
- Anaesthetised or unconscious? ⇒ the usual symptoms may not be reported
## Instructions Over the Phone
- Stop the transfusion, and keep the pack and giving set
- Keep IV access with a new set and normal saline, and don't flush the existing line
- Full set of observations, and record the volume and colour of any urine
- Call a clinical emergency if there are any ABC problems
# Aetiologies
- **Fever:** FNHTR, bacterial contamination, acute haemolytic reaction, or something unrelated to the transfusion
- **Rash or itch:** minor allergic reaction, [[Anaphylaxis]]
- **Hypotension or dyspnoea, usually within 15 minutes of starting:** bacterial contamination, acute haemolytic reaction, anaphylaxis
- **Respiratory distress during or after the transfusion:** TACO, TRALI
- **Anaesthetised:** hypotension, uncontrolled bleeding or oozing, and haemoglobinuria may be the first signs of an acute haemolytic reaction
# Assessment
- **Airway:** stridor or swelling ⇒ anaphylaxis
- **Breathing:** RR, SpO2, wheeze, and whether crackles are basal or bilateral
- **Circulation:** BP against baseline, HR, JVP, fluid balance, and any bleeding or oozing
- **Disability:** restlessness or a sense of impending doom can occur in acute haemolytic reactions
- **Exposure:** temperature, rash, pain at the infusion site, flank or back pain, and urine colour
- Check correct pack went into correct patient
## TACO or TRALI?
| | TACO | TRALI |
|---|---|---|
| Timing | Up to 12 hours | Within 6 hours |
| BP | Typically hypertensive | Typically hypotensive |
| JVP | Raised | Not elevated |
| Crackles | Basal | Bilateral |
| Fluid balance | Positive | No evidence of overload |
| Fever | Not typical | Can occur |
| Pro-BNP | Raised more often | Raised less often |
| Diuretics | Give | Not beneficial and may worsen it |
# Immediate Management
- Stop the transfusion, then assess ABC and vital signs
- Keep IV access, using a new line if needed and not flushing the existing one
- Repeat the identity and clerical checks, and inspect the pack for clots, haemolysis, discolouration or cloudiness
- Notify haematology and the transfusion service provider. The transfusion service provider will advise on returning the pack and on any further samples
- Further transfusion needs advice from the transfusion service provider, a transfusion medicine specialist or a haematologist. If it's needed before the lab has finished investigating the reaction (e.g. excluding a haemolytic reaction), a suitably experienced pathologist (lab director, haematologist or transfusion medicine specialist) has to authorise it
# By Reaction
- **Mild febrile:** >38°C and a rise of 1 to <1.5°C within 4 hours, with no other signs[^1]. Give an antipyretic and restart slowly only if it settles, otherwise don't restart
- **Moderate or severe febrile:** ≥39°C, a rise of ≥1.5°C or other signs such as rigors, hypotension or pain. Don't restart, and since FNHTR is a diagnosis of exclusion, work up for [[Sepsis|sepsis]], incompatibility and [[Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation|DIC]]
- **Acute haemolytic:** don't restart, support BP (vasopressors if needed, as there may be cytokine shock) and urine output to prevent haem-associated ATN, consider diuresis with IV fluids and diuretics[^2], and no more blood until haematology or the transfusion service provider clears it
- **Bacterial contamination:** don't restart, start broad-spectrum IV antibiotics (regimen from eTG) and support BP and urine output with fluids and inotropes
- **Isolated urticaria or pruritus:** give an antihistamine and restart slowly if it settles and the product is still viable
- **Generalised urticaria or localised angioedema:** don't restart, give an antihistamine and a corticosteroid as required, and watch for progression
- **[[Anaphylaxis]]:** don't restart, and follow the local anaphylaxis protocol including adrenaline. If IgA deficient with IgA antibodies, consider washed red cells and IgA deficient components
- **TACO:** during or up to 12 hours after transfusion[^3], mostly within 1-2 hours. Don't restart, pause IV fluids, sit them up, and give oxygen and diuretics. Consider a venodilator such as GTN to reduce preload, and a higher PEEP if ventilated
- **TRALI:** during or within 6 hours, usually within 1-2 hours. Don't restart, give oxygen and supportive care, and get expert input on ventilation, ventilating like [[Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome|ARDS]]. Diuretics aren't beneficial and may worsen it
# Investigations
- **Isolated urticaria or other minor allergic reactions:** none needed
- **[[Sepsis]]:** blood cultures (+/- central line), septic screen for other sources, and send the pack and giving set for culture and Gram stain
- **Incompatibility:** group, antibody screen and DAT on pre- and post-transfusion samples, repeat crossmatch, plus a haemolytic screen (FBC, LDH, bilirubin, haptoglobin, electrolytes, creatinine, urinalysis)
- **[[Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation|DIC]]:** aPTT, PT, fibrinogen, D-dimer
- **[[Anaphylaxis]]:** serial tryptase, IgA +/- IgA antibodies, haptoglobin. Tryptase is ideally taken 30 minutes to 2 hours after onset but can still be useful up to 6 hours, with a baseline from before the event or at least 24 hours after it resolves. A rise of at least 20% + 2 ng/mL over baseline suggests mast cell activation[^4]
- **TACO or TRALI:** CXR (looking for [[Acute Pulmonary Oedema]]) and pro-BNP, and consider an echo. For TRALI, discuss HLA/HNA typing with Lifeblood
# Delayed Reactions
- **Delayed haemolytic:** 2-14 days after exposure, typically from Kidd, Duffy or Kell antibodies
- **Post-transfusion purpura:** sudden thrombocytopenia 5-10 days after a blood or platelet transfusion. High-dose IV immunoglobulin seems to be the treatment of choice, with plasmapheresis as an option
- **Transfusion-associated GVHD:** 4-40 days after transfusion, with a widespread rash and desquamation first, then GI mucositis and hepatitis. Almost uniformly fatal, and irradiating components reduces donor T lymphocytes
- Consider seeking advice from Lifeblood for any of these
# Large-Volume Transfusion
- **[[Hypocalcaemia]]:** citrate binds calcium, so replace it
- **[[Hyperkalaemia]]:** from the high potassium content of stored red cells, so treat as usual
- **Hypothermia:** from refrigerated products, so rewarm aggressively and use a blood warmer
- **Dilutional coagulopathy and thrombocytopenia:** FFP and cryoprecipitate initially, and platelets
# Preventing TACO
- Do a risk assessment before transfusing, give single units with review in between, transfuse slowly and monitor fluid balance
- ANZSBT says to consider diuretics in at-risk patients, although I couldn't find an agent, dose or timing in the Australian sources I read. AABB says there isn't enough data to support or refute it
# Reporting in NSW
- All suspected reactions: report to the pathology lab as per local procedure and enter into ims+, and it then gets tabled at the Blood Management Committee
- Suspected transfusion-transmitted infection or TRALI: report to the Lifeblood 24-hour hotline on 1300 478 348
- Plasma-derived products: report to Lifeblood and the TGA
- ABO haemolytic reaction causing serious harm or death: this is a sentinel event, so needs a Reportable Incident Brief
